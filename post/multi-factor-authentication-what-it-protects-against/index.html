Multi-Factor Authentication: What It Protects Against — and What It Doesn't

May 27, 2026 · AegisPub

Multi-factor authentication is probably the most widely recommended cybersecurity control in existence. Security teams recommend it. Government agencies mandate it. Breach post-mortems cite its absence as a contributing factor again and again.

And yet there’s considerable confusion about what MFA actually does, how it can be bypassed, and why context matters as much as the factor itself.

This article explains how MFA works, what it protects against, and how Zero Trust architecture extends it beyond its traditional form.

What MFA Actually Does

At its core, MFA requires more than one proof of identity before granting access. The underlying principle is factor diversity: combine something you know (a password), something you have (a physical device or authentication app), and something you are (biometric data like a fingerprint or face scan).

The security value is straightforward. Passwords get stolen. They’re phished, guessed, leaked in data breaches, reused across sites. A stolen password is a common and well-documented problem.

What’s harder to steal simultaneously is the second factor — specifically, a time-sensitive code generated by an authentication app on your physical phone, or a hardware security key in your pocket. An attacker who has your password but not your phone cannot complete authentication. The stolen credential is insufficient on its own.

This is why the security community pushes MFA so consistently. The majority of credential-based account compromises would have been stopped if MFA had been enabled. It’s not a complete solution. It’s a highly effective partial one.

What MFA Doesn’t Protect Against

Understanding MFA’s limitations is as important as understanding its protections.

Real-time phishing attacks. Sophisticated phishing attacks don’t just capture your password — they capture your MFA code too, in real time. The attacker’s website asks for your credentials, immediately relays them to the legitimate site, receives the MFA prompt, relays that to you, you enter your code, the attacker receives it, and uses it before it expires.

This is why security-conscious organizations increasingly prefer phishing-resistant MFA factors: hardware security keys (FIDO2/WebAuthn) that are cryptographically bound to specific sites and cannot be relayed to a different site, and passkeys that work similarly.

App-based authenticator codes (TOTP) are significantly better than no MFA, but they are not phishing-resistant in the way hardware keys are.

Malware on the device. If the device used to generate MFA codes is itself compromised with malware, the attacker may be able to intercept codes at the point of generation. This is relatively rare but technically feasible.

Social engineering (SIM swapping). SMS-based MFA — receiving a code via text message — is the weakest form of MFA and is vulnerable to SIM-swapping attacks, where an attacker convinces a carrier to transfer your phone number to a SIM they control. They then receive your MFA codes.

SMS MFA is substantially better than no MFA. But it should be replaced with an authenticator app or hardware key wherever possible, particularly for high-sensitivity accounts.

MFA in the Context of Zero Trust

Traditional MFA is a perimeter-based control: you prove two factors at the front door, and you’re trusted for the session.

Zero Trust extends MFA into the continuous verification model. Instead of passing once at login, authentication strength is evaluated dynamically based on context throughout a session.

Risk-based authentication works like this: the policy engine maintains a risk score for each access request based on multiple signals. When the risk score is low (familiar device, familiar location, normal working hours, accessing a routine application), the user’s existing authentication is considered sufficient.

When the risk score elevates — unfamiliar device, unusual location, sensitive application, anomalous access time, behavior deviating from historical patterns — the policy engine requires step-up authentication. The user is prompted to verify again with their strongest available factor before access is granted.

This step-up is invisible when it’s not triggered and seamless when it is. A developer accessing the code repository at 10am from their usual laptop experiences no friction. The same developer attempting to access the production infrastructure console from an unfamiliar IP at 2am faces an additional verification challenge before the proxy permits the request.

The friction is proportional to the risk signal. Legitimate users doing ordinary things experience security as invisible. Unusual access patterns — which is what an attacker using stolen credentials typically creates — experience it as demanding.

Practical Guidance by Account Sensitivity

Not all accounts carry equal risk. Prioritizing MFA deployment by account sensitivity helps organizations focus effort where it matters most.

Critical priority (implement immediately if not done): Administrative accounts for any system — email, cloud platforms, financial software, IT infrastructure. The compromise of one administrative account can affect the entire organization. No exceptions to MFA here.

Email accounts. For most organizations, email is the starting point for all other attacks — password resets, phishing, access to sensitive communications. MFA on email is non-negotiable.

Financial and payment systems. Any account with the ability to move money or access financial data should require MFA from every user, always.

High priority: All employee accounts for core business applications, particularly those containing customer data or sensitive internal information. Remote access tools (VPN, remote desktop). Cloud platform accounts (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud).

Moderate priority: Internal collaboration tools, project management, lower-sensitivity productivity software.

Implementation Notes

When deploying MFA across an organization, a few practical considerations reduce friction and increase adoption:

Use an authenticator app (Microsoft Authenticator, Google Authenticator, Authy) rather than SMS for all accounts where supported. Authenticator apps are more secure and more reliable.

Consider hardware security keys (YubiKey and similar) for the highest-sensitivity accounts and roles — executives, IT administrators, financial personnel. Hardware keys are phishing-resistant and provide the strongest available protection.

Plan for device loss. Every MFA deployment needs a recovery path for users who lose their authentication device. Define the recovery process before it’s needed, not during the crisis.

Communicate the why to employees. MFA adoption improves significantly when people understand why it’s required. “We require this because it stops the most common type of account hijacking” is more motivating than “security requires it.”

Conclusion

Multi-factor authentication is not a complete solution. No single control is. But it is one of the most consistently effective controls available against the most common attack vector in cybersecurity: the stolen or guessed credential.

Deployed with an understanding of its limitations, integrated into a broader access control model that continuously evaluates context, and combined with scoped authorization that limits what compromised accounts can reach — MFA is the foundation most organizations should build everything else on.

If it’s not yet enabled everywhere in your organization, start there. Today.
