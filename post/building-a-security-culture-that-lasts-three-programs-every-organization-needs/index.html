Building a Security Culture That Lasts: Three Programs Every Organization Needs

October 6, 2026 · AegisPub

Introduction

Security awareness training is not a security culture. It is a component of one. The distinction matters because organizations that mistake the training program for the culture tend to stop building at exactly the point where the real work begins.

A security culture is the set of norms, habits, and relationships that shape how an organization responds to security challenges across the full range of situations it encounters — including the situations that no training module anticipated. Building it requires three structural elements working in parallel: an awareness program designed for behavioral change, a champion network that distributes security competency throughout the organization, and a recognition and reporting program that makes the right behaviors visible and valuable.

None of these is complicated. All of them require sustained attention.

Element One: Awareness Training Built for Behavioral Change

The purpose of security awareness training is behavioral change. Not knowledge transfer.

This distinction has enormous practical consequences. Telling someone that phishing emails are dangerous does not reliably make them better at identifying phishing emails when they are reading their hundredth email of the day, under time pressure, with five meetings on the calendar. Knowledge does not translate automatically into behavior in high-stress, high-volume conditions.

What produces behavioral change is repeated practice with immediate, specific feedback in conditions that approximate reality.

This is why phishing simulations work better than phishing videos — but only when done correctly. A simulation that runs the same type of lure four times a year produces employees who are skilled at recognizing that one lure while remaining completely unprepared for others. A simulation program that varies lure categories — executive impersonation, IT helpdesk requests, delivery notifications, invoice approvals — produces broader vigilance.

The feedback timing matters as much as the content. The training delivered at the moment an employee clicks a simulated phishing link is the most effective training they will receive. Not because it embarrasses them, but because it arrives at exactly the moment when their brain is processing why they made that decision.

Structuring training for new hires. The first week of employment is a poor time for detailed security content. New employees are absorbing an enormous amount of information simultaneously. Security content delivered in week one should focus on context — what the organization protects and why it matters — not rules and restrictions. In the first month, training should be grounded in the new employee’s actual role. By the end of the first quarter, they should have encountered a simulation, received specific feedback, and had the opportunity to ask questions based on real experience rather than hypothetical scenarios.

Element Two: Security Champions — Distributed Competency

The challenge with centralized security teams is that they are remote from the daily operational realities of most departments. A policy communicated by someone who has never attended a client meeting looks different from the same policy explained by a peer who was in the last three.

A security champion program addresses this by creating a network of security-aware people throughout the organization: individuals in non-security roles who receive additional training, stay current on emerging threats, and serve as the first point of contact for security questions and concerns in their department.

Selecting champions correctly. Champions are most effective when they self-select based on genuine interest, not when they are appointed because someone needed to send a body to security training. The right candidate is already asking security questions informally. Look for curiosity, not compliance.

Sustaining the program. Champions who receive no follow-up after their initial training drift away from the role within six months. Regular briefings — ideally monthly or biweekly — that share relevant intelligence keep champions current and make them feel like part of the security program rather than volunteers being used for overhead.

Element Three: Recognition and Reporting — Making Right Behaviors Visible

Most organizations have an incentive structure that inadvertently suppresses security reporting. Security failures get attention and consequences. Security successes — the reported phishing email, the flagged suspicious request — are invisible. The employee who is not sure whether something is suspicious and stays quiet faces no consequence for the silence.

Inverting this structure requires a recognition program that makes the right behaviors visible and valued — and a reporting feedback loop that makes reporting feel like a two-way interaction rather than a one-way action.

Recognition that works. The most effective recognition is specific, immediate, and frequent — not a general annual award, but a specific acknowledgment shortly after the behavior. Frequency matters more than ceremony.

The reporting feedback loop. Every reported suspicious email should generate an acknowledgment within fifteen minutes of submission — not a resolution, just confirmation that the report was received and is being reviewed. Within twenty-four hours, the reporter should receive a response explaining what the email was and why the verdict was what it was.

When the Program Is Not Working: Diagnosing Honestly

Three persistent failure patterns signal that the program needs redesign rather than intensification.

Stagnant phishing simulation click rates. A click rate that does not improve despite repeated simulations and training usually indicates that the simulation program is testing a narrow range of attack types. Expand the lure variety and analyze results by category rather than as a blended rate.

Reports that go unacknowledged. If employees report suspicious emails and receive no response, they stop reporting. Automated acknowledgment within fifteen minutes and verdict feedback within twenty-four hours are the minimum feedback loop needed to sustain reporting behavior.

Senior leaders who refuse training. This is a governance signal, not just a compliance problem. The training may genuinely be misaligned with their risk profile. The solution is a curriculum redesigned for their actual risk, not enforced participation in training that does not apply to them.

Conclusion

A security culture that lasts is not built on annual training videos and signed policy acknowledgments. It is built on the sustained interaction of three elements: a training program that changes behavior rather than transferring knowledge, a champion network that brings security competency into every department, and a recognition and reporting system that makes security behaviors visible and valued.

Building them takes time and sustained attention. It also produces something that no policy can: an organization where security works because people want it to, not because they are required to.
